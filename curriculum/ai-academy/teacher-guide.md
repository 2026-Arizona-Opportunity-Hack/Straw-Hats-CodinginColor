# AI Academy teacher guide

Facilitator notes for the classroom course.
They live here, not in the lessons, because students can open every lesson activity.

The five interactive pages (Build the Machine, Binary Letters, Then vs. Now, AI or Not AI?, AI App Investigation) are in the classroom course too.
Project one for the whole class, or let students open it on their own devices before the matching worksheet.

## Intro to Computers & AI

### Welcome & class norms

**Time:** about 15 minutes. Slides 1-5 of the Week 1 deck.

**1. Welcome (2 min).** Introduce AI Academy: Intro to Computers & Artificial Intelligence.

**2. Meet Your Classmates (8 min).** Students find someone they do not know well.
They learn a first name, grade, one interest, and one problem the person hopes technology could help solve.
Each student introduces their partner out loud.
The lesson worksheet only asks for the interest and the problem.
Remind students not to write last names, addresses or other personal details.

**3. How We'll Learn Together (3 min).** Read the norms together:

- Be curious - questions are always welcome.
- Try before saying, "I can't."
- Help your team without taking over.
- Critique ideas, not people.
- Use AI honestly and protect personal information.
- Make something you are proud to share.

**4. Course overview (2 min).** Six modules: computers, AI history and common AI tools, Scratch and Python, data and prediction, ethics and social good, and a community AI capstone.

**Check answer:** "Leave out the address and use made-up details" (choice b). It matches the norm about protecting personal information.

Note: students in your class can open this page too. Delete it from your copy if you prefer.

### Pretest

**Time:** about 10 minutes. Slides 6-7 of the Week 1 deck.

1. Students fill out CIC's Pre Survey (link in the next activity).
2. Students take the in-app pretest. It replaces the Pre Assessment form on slide 7, so you do not need both.

Tell students the pretest is not graded. It shows what they already know so the class can start in the right place.
The posttest at the end of the module has matching questions, so you can see growth on the class dashboard.

Pretest topics: computer parts, binary, early vs modern computers, what AI is, AI or not, how AI learns, and AI limits.

### What is a computer?

**Time:** about 30 minutes. Slides 9-18 of the Week 1 deck.

**1. Raise your hand (2 min).** Ask "What is a computer?" and collect a few answers.

**2. Definition (3 min).** A computer is an electronic device that processes data and performs tasks based on instructions.
Computers follow programs to process data. They calculate, store information, connect to networks, and help people communicate.

**3. Videos.** Play "What is a computer?" (slide 12) and "Inside a computer" (slide 15) from the deck.

**4. Build the Machine (about 15 min).** Groups of 3-4. Use the lesson worksheet.

- Prepare cards for keyboard/mouse (input), CPU (processing), RAM (working memory), SSD or hard drive (storage), and screen/speaker (output).
- Research (10 min): each group investigates its assigned part.
- Explain: write the part's job and one example on a notecard.
- Connect: arrange input, CPU and output in a line. Place memory and storage alongside the CPU, not as a step after it.
- Test: trace typing and saving a word.

Memory and storage exchange information with the CPU. Storage does not have to be a separate step after every calculation.

**5. Recap (5 min).** Input brings data in. The CPU follows instructions. RAM holds data and instructions in use. An SSD or hard drive keeps files when the power is off. Output shows the result. Computers represent all data with bits (0 and 1).
Avoid the old picture that puts memory inside the CPU.

**Check answers:** CPU follows instructions (b). Everything is stored as 0s and 1s (c). The SSD keeps files when power is off (a).

### Then vs. now

**Time:** about 25 minutes. Slides 19-28 of the Week 1 deck.

**1. Raise your hand (2 min).** "Does anyone know what the oldest computer is?"

**2. Early and modern computing (4 min).** Show the Difference Engine (gears) next to a modern processor (electronic circuits).
Pairs discuss: what changed in size, speed, and possible tasks?
Takeaway: both automate calculation with physical parts, but a modern computer can run many different programs.

**Correct facts (important).** Slide 21 contains errors. Use these facts instead:

- Charles Babbage proposed the first Difference Engine in 1822.
- It was a mechanical calculating engine, not a modern general-purpose computer.
- It was not a completed steam-powered machine, and it was not "the first mechanical computer".
- The five-ton figure is the later Difference Engine No. 2 reconstruction, not the 1822 design.

Sources: [Whipple Museum](https://www.whipplemuseum.cam.ac.uk/explore-whipple-collections/calculating-devices/charles-babbages-difference-engine), [Computer History Museum: the engines](https://www.computerhistory.org/babbage/engines/), [Computer History Museum: overview](https://www.computerhistory.org/babbage/overview/).

**3. Videos.** Play "Early computer" (slide 22) and "Coin sorter" (slide 24) from the deck.
Coin sorter analogy: both machines take an input (mixed coins), process it mechanically, and give an output (sorted coins).

**4. Then vs. Now (10 min).** Use the lesson worksheet.

- Research (7 min): Group A picks an early computer (ENIAC, the Difference Engine, an early IBM mainframe, or the Apollo Guidance Computer). Group B picks a device they use.
- Record size, speed or tasks, and input method.
- Pair across groups (3 min): share one difference and one similarity.

**5. Recap and discussion (4 min).** Modern computers are much smaller, faster and more powerful. Early computers did basic calculations and needed manual input through punch cards or switches.
Ask: "What computer do you use every day that is not a phone, tablet or desktop?" (Examples: game console, smart TV, car dashboard, microwave, calculator.)

**Check answers:** the Difference Engine was a mechanical calculator with gears (d). Modern computers can run many different programs (b).

### What is AI?

**Time:** about 45 minutes. Slides 29-38 of the Week 1 deck.

**1. How do you think AI works? (3 min).** Each student writes one idea and one everyday example on a sticky note, compares with a partner, then shares.
Collect ideas without grading them. Revisit the sticky notes after the video.

**2. Working definition (2 min).** Artificial intelligence is a broad field of computer systems that perform tasks such as recognizing patterns, understanding language, or making predictions.
Many current AI systems learn patterns from training examples and use those patterns on new inputs. Not every AI system uses machine learning.

**3. AI or Not AI? (13 min).** Use the lesson worksheet. Sort the examples (10 min) with one reason each, then compare answers (3 min): what evidence helped?

Answer guidance under ordinary assumptions:

- Basic calculator, simple light switch, basic vending machine: **Not AI**. They follow fixed instructions.
- Netflix recommendations, Siri, a self-driving system: **AI**.
- Thermostat: **Depends**. A basic thermostat follows a temperature threshold. A learning thermostat may use AI.

Calculators, switches and vending machines can be parts of larger AI-enabled systems, so accept a conditional answer when students explain how it actually works.
Automation alone is not evidence of AI. Discuss reasoning rather than treating brand labels as proof.

**4. Video (5 min).** Code.org "What is Machine Learning?" (in the lesson). Afterwards ask: what do examples help a machine learn, and why can examples lead to mistakes?
Series context: [Code.org How AI Works](https://code.org/en-US/curriculum/how-artificial-intelligence-works).

**5. What can AI do? (3 min, partners).** Pairs choose a task: recommending a movie, identifying a photo, or drafting a message. What information would the AI need? What could it produce? Where could it make a mistake?
Suggested answers: recommendations use information about preferences and other patterns. Image classifiers use image information to predict labels. Language systems draft text from a prompt and learned patterns.
Limitations: factual errors, bias, missing context, unreliable outputs. More data does not automatically improve an AI system.
Ask students what they would check before trusting an output.

**6. AI Scavenger Hunt (about 13 min).** Use the lesson worksheet. Five groups: smart assistants, recommendation systems, chatbots, facial recognition, navigation apps.

- 3 min: each student researches one real example: how it works plus a surprising fact.
- 3-5 min: share within the group and plan a short group lesson.
- 5 min: teach the class. Everyone presents one example.

Students use their own words and name their source.

**7. Group discussion (2 min).** "What AI applications do you use in your everyday life?"

**Check answers:** examples help a machine learn patterns (a). A learning thermostat may use AI; a basic one does not (c). Check AI output with a trusted source (d). Navigation apps predicting traffic use AI (b).

### AI App Investigation

**Time:** 35 minutes, plus about 10 minutes of sharing. Slides 39-45 of the Week 1 deck.

**Objective:** research and test an AI application.

1. Students choose one AI application that your school allows. Check each app's age rules; many chatbots require users to be 13 or older.
2. They record its purpose and three key features.
3. They test one fact-checking prompt, one creative prompt, and one capability question (the prompt lists are in the project instructions).
4. They summarize the responses, then name one strength and one limitation.
5. They prepare a one-minute comparison for the class.

Students turn in the lesson worksheet instead of a separate page or shared document.
Remind them never to type personal information (full name, address, school name, photos) into an AI tool.

**Let's hear your findings (10 min).** Ask volunteers to share their one-minute comparison.
Good discussion points: the capability questions ("Do you have access to real-time news?", "Who won the Super Bowl last weekend?") often show that a tool's knowledge has a cutoff date or that it can sound sure and still be wrong.

**Grading (10 points):** app and purpose, three features, three prompts with summaries, one strength, one limitation, and one question.

### Wrap-up

**Time:** about 10 minutes. Slide 46 of the Week 1 deck.

1. **Exit question (3 min).** Students answer it in the app.
2. **Posttest (5 min).** Its questions match the pretest topics, so the dashboard can compare each student's pretest and posttest by concept.
3. **Next up (1 min).** Preview Module 2: AI History and Common AI Technologies.

If a student scores well on the pretest, they may already have tested out of this module, depending on your class's pass mark.
